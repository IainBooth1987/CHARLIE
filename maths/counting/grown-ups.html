Charlie's Learning · Counting — a guide for grown-ups

What's really going on when children count?

Saying "one, two, three…" is only part of counting. Researchers Gelman and Gallistel described the big ideas children need to put together. Each game here practises one of them:

One for each thing (one-to-one)
Every object gets exactly one number — no skipping, no counting twice.
Same order every time (stable order)
The number words always go one, two, three… in the same order.
The last number tells you how many (cardinal)
After counting 1-2-3-4-5, "five" is the answer to "how many?" — this is the big milestone.
Count anything, in any order
Toys, claps, steps or jumps can all be counted, and starting somewhere else gives the same total.

What's expected in Reception?

Development Matters (the Department for Education's guidance for Reception) says children will be learning to:

  • count objects, actions and sounds
  • count beyond ten
  • link the number symbol (numeral) with how many it stands for

By the end of Reception, the Early Learning Goal is to verbally count beyond 20, recognising the pattern of the counting system.

How you can help

While they play

💬 After they count: "So how many are there?" If they count again from the start, that's normal — say "you said five last, so there are five!"
  • Encourage touching or tapping each one — and moving counted things to one side helps them keep track.
  • Let them count in any order; the answer stays the same.
  • Count the beats together out loud at first, then let them try quietly.

Away from the screen

  • Count everything: stairs as you climb, grapes on the plate, jumps on the trampoline.
  • "Give me…": "Can you give me 4 spoons?" is the real-life version of Count Out.
  • Claps and jumps: "How many claps did I do?", "Do 5 jumps!"
  • Count past ten: count down from 20 at bath time, or count steps on a walk — pause at 10 and 20 to notice the pattern.
  • Hide and seek: counting to 20 while hiding your eyes is perfect practice.
