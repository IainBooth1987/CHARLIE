Charlie's Learning · Subitising — a guide for grown-ups

What is subitising?

Subitising is knowing how many things there are just by looking, without counting them one by one. It's what you do when you glance at a dice and know it's a five. The word comes from the Latin for suddenly.

Seeing it straight away (perceptual subitising)
Spotting a small amount instantly — is "three" without counting. This works for amounts up to about 5, for children and adults alike.
Seeing it in parts (conceptual subitising)
Bigger amounts are seen as smaller groups put together — is "five and two more, so seven".

Seeing numbers as parts that make a whole is one of the biggest steps in early maths. It leads straight into number bonds, adding and taking away.

What's expected by the end of Reception?

The Early Years Foundation Stage goal for Number says children at the expected level will:

  • subitise (recognise quantities without counting) up to 5
  • have a deep understanding of numbers to 10, including how each number is made — for example, 7 as 5 and 2, or 4 and 3
  • recall number bonds to 5, and some to 10

So in this game, amounts 1–5 are about just seeing, and 6–10 are about seeing five and some more.

How the levels build up

The levels follow the advice from NRICH, Third Space Learning and BBC Bitesize: start with small groups (1–3), then up to 5, and only then bigger numbers seen in parts. At each size there is a look-and-check level, where the picture stays and children can tap to check, followed by a quick look, where it hides so they have to see the number rather than count it.

Game 2 — Same or Different?  ·  Game 3 — Make It

These never hide the picture. Their levels go 1–3, 1–5, 6–10 and a mix. Same or Different? shows the same amount in different patterns — and often with different things (rockets and stars, or pictures and dots) — so children learn that 4 is still 4 whatever it is and however it's arranged. Make It uses a ten frame (two rows of five), the same tool used in Reception classrooms.

When to move on: the game marks the next level with a yellow "NEXT" once your child gets 2 or more stars. If they're pointing at and counting each picture on a quick-look level, go back a level — that's completely normal.

Pictures or dots?

NRICH points out that everyday objects are harder to subitise than simple dots, but children are often more interested in them. So the game mixes both: most rounds use the theme pictures (stars, rockets, planets), and every few rounds uses plain dots. The answer cards always show dice-style dots, linking the picture to the pattern.

You can change this in the grown-ups settings (press and hold the 🔒 Grown-ups button in a game): mix, theme pictures only or simple dots only. If your child finds the pictures hard, try dots only for a while.

How you can help

While they play

💬 "How did you know?" or "What did you see?" — the game asks this too. "I saw two and one" is a brilliant answer.
  • Accept different answers about how they saw it — there's no single right way.
  • If they still count to check, don't worry — it's a hard idea and takes time. Look together and talk about what you both see.
  • Start with long looks. In the grown-ups settings you can set the look time, or choose "Always stay on".
  • If the numbers on the answer cards are new to them, set the cards to "Dots only".
  • Rounds are short (8 questions) with a movement break in the middle. Stopping after a round or two is ideal.

Away from the screen

  • Fingers: "Show me 3!" — all at once, not counted up one by one. For bigger numbers: "a whole hand and two more".
  • Dice and dominoes: board games like snakes and ladders build instant recognition of dot patterns.
  • Quick looks: put a few toys or pasta shapes under a tea towel, lift it for a second, then ask how many.
  • Egg-box ten frame: cut two rows of five from an egg box and fill it with small toys — "how many? how many empty?"
  • Coloured bricks: put out 3 bricks — 2 blue and 1 red. "Can you see 3 bricks? Can you see 2 blue ones and 1 red one?" This is seeing a number in parts, with toys they already love.
  • Out and about: two ducks on a pond, three cars in a row — "how many? no counting!"
  • Clap it: clap 3 times and ask how many claps — subitising works for sounds too.
  • Use their interests: cars, dinosaurs, stickers — the same skill, with things they love.
